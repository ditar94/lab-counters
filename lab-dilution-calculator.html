Laboratory Dilution Calculator

Calculate dilution ratios like 1:5 and 1:10, and C1V1=C2V2 concentration dilutions, for routine laboratory use.

This free laboratory dilution calculator covers two routine jobs on the bench: splitting a final volume into specimen and diluent according to a dilution ratio (such as 1:5 or 1:10), and working out a concentration dilution using C1V1 = C2V2. Results update instantly as you type — no "Calculate" button needed. Dilution-ratio notation is a common source of error, so this tool makes the convention you're using explicit at every step.

Mode A: Dilution Ratio Calculator

A dilution ratio like "1:5" is written the same way under two different, incompatible conventions. This tool asks you explicitly which one you mean, so your specimen and diluent volumes always come out correctly.

Convention 1 — "1 in X" (1 part specimen in X total parts): a 1:5 dilution means 1 part specimen + 4 parts diluent = 5 total parts.
Convention 2 — "1 plus X" (1 part specimen plus X parts diluent): a 1:5 dilution means 1 part specimen + 5 parts diluent = 6 total parts.
These give different volumes for the same "1:5" label — always confirm which convention your protocol uses. Convention 1 is selected by default below.

What This Calculator Does

This is a general-purpose dilution calculator and laboratory dilution calculator for two of the most common dilution questions on the bench: converting a dilution ratio calculator request like "1:5" or "1:10 dilution calculator" into actual specimen and diluent volumes, and solving a C1V1 C2V2 calculator problem for mass concentrations (mg/mL, µg/mL, ng/mL). It functions as a straightforward sample dilution calculator for a single dilution step.

When Laboratory Technologists Use It

Dilution ratios come up constantly — diluting a reagent, a specimen, or a control to a working strength before testing. The trouble is that "1:5" is written identically for two different real-world dilutions depending on which convention a protocol follows, which is a frequent source of bench errors. This tool makes the convention explicit and shows the resulting volumes for whichever one you select, alongside a simple proportional visual.

How the Formulas Work

Mode A — Dilution Ratio

Convention 1 (1 in X total parts): Specimen = Final volume ÷ X, Diluent = Final volume − Specimen.
Convention 2 (1 part specimen + X parts diluent): Specimen = Final volume ÷ (1 + X), Diluent = Final volume − Specimen.

Mode B — Concentration Dilution

C1 × V1 = C2 × V2, rearranged to V1 = (C2 × V2) / C1, with Diluent = V2 − V1.

Worked Examples

Example — Dilution Ratio, Convention 1

A 1:5 final dilution under Convention 1 means 1 part specimen + 4 parts diluent. For a 1,000 µL final volume: specimen = 1,000 ÷ 5 = 200 µL, diluent = 800 µL.

Example — Dilution Ratio, Convention 2

A 1:5 specimen-to-diluent dilution under Convention 2 means 1 part specimen + 5 parts diluent (6 total parts). For a 1,200 µL final volume: specimen = 1,200 ÷ 6 = 200 µL, diluent = 1,000 µL.

Example — Concentration Dilution

Starting concentration: 10 mg/mL. Target concentration: 2 mg/mL. Final volume: 500 µL.
V1 = (2 × 500) ÷ 10 = 100 µL stock. Diluent = 500 − 100 = 400 µL diluent.

Common Mistakes

  • Confusing the two ratio conventions. "1:5" means a different real dilution depending on whether it's read as "1 in 5 total parts" or "1 part plus 5 parts diluent." Always confirm which your protocol intends.
  • Treating a dilution factor and a dilution ratio as interchangeable without checking. A "5-fold dilution" and a "1:5 dilution" are usually the same thing under Convention 1, but not under Convention 2 — read labels carefully.
  • Mixing concentration units between C1 and C2. This tool uses one shared unit for both; converting only one side by hand is a common source of 1000-fold errors.
  • Assuming a higher target concentration is reachable by dilution. Dilution can only lower concentration — a target above the starting concentration requires concentrating the sample, not diluting it.
  • Treating this as a serial dilution planner. This tool calculates one dilution step at a time; it does not generate a serial dilution series.

Units & Assumptions

Dilution ratio mode works in µL or mL and is unit-agnostic otherwise — the ratio applies regardless of volume unit. Concentration mode supports mg/mL, µg/mL, and ng/mL, converted internally to a common base unit before the C1V1=C2V2 calculation is performed. Both modes assume accurate pipetting and a well-mixed final solution; neither mode plans a multi-step serial dilution.

Frequently Asked Questions

What's the difference between the two dilution-ratio conventions?
Convention 1 treats "1:5" as 1 part specimen in 5 total parts (4 parts diluent). Convention 2 treats "1:5" as 1 part specimen plus 5 parts diluent (6 total parts). They produce different volumes for the same label, which is exactly why this tool asks you to pick one explicitly.
Which convention should I use?
Whichever your protocol or reference method specifies. If you're unsure, Convention 1 ("1 in X total parts") is the more common usage in clinical laboratory serology and general dilution language, and is selected by default here — but always confirm against your SOP.
Does this calculator plan a serial dilution series?
No. It solves one dilution step at a time; it does not generate or plan a multi-step serial dilution.
Can I use different units for C1 and C2 in the concentration mode?
The current interface uses one shared unit for both C1 and C2. Choose the unit dropdown that matches your reagent's labeling.
Does this replace manual verification in my lab?
No. Treat results as a calculation aid and verify them against your institution's SOPs before use, as noted in the disclaimer below.
